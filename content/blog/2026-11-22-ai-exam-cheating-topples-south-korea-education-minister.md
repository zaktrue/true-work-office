A candidate arriving for South Korea's suneung hands over a phone, submits glasses and watches for inspection, and sits an eight-hour paper in a hall where the public wireless network has been switched off. Those checks were imposed at speed in November 2026, after the top scorer in a national tax accountant examination admitted using a miniature camera and a smartphone to reach a near-perfect result, four days before the university entrance exam.

The political cost landed on 22 November, when education minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned and President Lee Jae Myung accepted his departure. Choi had told a parliamentary hearing that, at 72, he was not confident of catching determined cheats, and he stayed away from the press conferences before the exam while a vice minister announced the emergency measures. Officials logged 111 cases of electronic device misconduct during the Thursday examination, nearly half of them caught by unsilenceable alerts pushed to every phone in the country. [The Guardian's account of the resignation and the exam security measures](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/22/south-korea-education-minister-resigns-after-ai-cheating-scandal-engulfs-university-exams) sets out how quickly a single confession moved a national assessment system into emergency procedure.

What is new is less the existence of cheating than the shape of the answer: a country's mobile alert infrastructure repurposed as a detection grid, and a ministerial career ended by fraud committed in a professional examination upstream of the school system. The measures operate at the perimeter, checking bodies and devices at the door. They leave untouched the assumption underneath, that one timed paper, taken once, can stand as proof of years of study.

## Evidence rather than equipment

That assumption is where honest use of AI turns from slogan into design problem. Declared tools, recorded process, drafts retained with version history and an oral defence of submitted work all generate evidence that a candidate can reproduce their own reasoning under questioning. Equipment bans generate compliance. They cannot separate a candidate who understands the material from one who has learned to conceal a device, and they spread inconvenience across every honest candidate in the room.

Verifiable practice also means being specific about what assistance was permitted and what a student did without it. A department that says only that AI is banned has written a rule, not a standard, and cannot show what a submitted grade actually represents. The South Korean case makes the gap visible at national scale, in a system where a single score decides which university a candidate may attend.

For institutions still building their integrity policy around a single sit-down examination, the practical work is specifying what a candidate must be able to demonstrate, and under what conditions. What evidence of a candidate's own reasoning would a department need before it could defend a grade as its own?
