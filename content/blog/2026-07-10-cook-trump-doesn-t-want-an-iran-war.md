When a senior Middle East analyst is asked, on a flagship financial programme, whether the United States is sliding back into full-scale war with Iran, his answer matters less for its certainty than for the clarity of the gap it draws. According to [Bloomberg Markets](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-07-10/trump-does-not-want-all-out-war-in-iran-cook-says-video), Steven Cook, a senior fellow for Middle East studies at the Council on Foreign Relations, told Bloomberg Surveillance on 10 July 2026 that President Donald Trump does not want a return to all-out combat with Iran. The same interview makes plain, though, that no one in view can say how the administration intends to "finish the job" once the latest round of strikes has run its course.

That combination, a clear stated preference against escalation alongside a deliberately under-specified endgame, is what caught our attention. On Cook's reading, the most likely path is restraint rather than a wider conflict, and that is the read markets, regional ministries and oil traders will weigh against the alternative for some time. What strikes us more, though, is the methodological posture on display: a respected analyst, on a high-traffic channel, naming what he can and cannot conclude, and resisting the temptation to fill the silence with confident speculation. The segment, under thirty seconds long, leaves the gap open rather than papering over it with assertion.

This is, in a different register, a problem we recognise from our own work on AI in education. The temptation in any fast-moving policy story, foreign or domestic, is to claim too much certainty too early: to pronounce that a tool "works" or that a generation is "ruined", to declare an outcome inevitable when it is barely visible. Cook's discipline on air is a useful counter-example, and the more honest move, and the one we try to make in our own writing on classroom AI, is to mark the boundary between evidence and inference, and to let readers see where that line sits. We think that habit is overdue in many of the louder conversations about AI in schools and universities, where confident assertions often travel further than the underlying data warrants.

A clear preference against war is welcome news in itself, but the harder question is what comes next, and on that the available evidence is thinner than the headlines. Whether the stated restraint holds at the next pressure point is, for now, an open question, and the same can be said of the louder conversations about AI in education, where a little more of the care we heard on a Bloomberg interview this week would not go amiss.
