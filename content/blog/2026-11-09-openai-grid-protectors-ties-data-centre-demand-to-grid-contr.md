On 2 November 2026 OpenAI announced Grid Protectors, a national programme meant to turn its future compute campuses into what it calls flexible electricity customers. Under [OpenAI's description of the Grid Protectors Program](https://openai.com/index/grid-protectors-program/), the company says it will work with utilities, regulators and policymakers on arrangements that let a campus cut its power draw when the grid is under strain. In some locations it would also supply electricity from on-site generation or storage.

The backdrop is scale. US data centre capacity is projected to exceed 100 gigawatts by 2030, and the company argues that artificial intelligence's growing share of electricity demand should not be subsidised by households and small businesses. The pilot sits in Effingham County, Georgia, under an agreement with Georgia Power. The company says it will cover the full infrastructure and electric-service costs of the project and provide demand response of up to 1,000 megawatts, roughly the average draw of 700,000 households. Georgia Power projects about $950 million a year in customer savings from 2029 across its large-load agreements, including Project Camellia, which it says would amount to at least $15 a month for a typical residential customer.

Two kinds of claim are being made here, and they carry different evidential weight. Demand response is an operational commitment: a campus either reduces its draw when asked or it does not, and metering can establish which. The savings figure is a forecast produced by a utility with an interest in the large-load deals it is defending. Only later billing data can settle it. Both deserve watching on their own terms, rather than being merged into a single story about good corporate behaviour.

The accountability sits in contract detail the announcement does not supply. Long-term agreements and financial assurances are said to protect other customers if a project is delayed, reduced or cancelled. Whether they do depends on penalty levels, on how curtailment is verified, on whether the obligation survives a change of ownership, and on how often the grid actually needs the campus to stand down. A headline capacity of 1,000 megawatts means little if the events that trigger it are rare, or if the measurement is left to the party with an incentive to report favourably.

The discipline here is familiar. Claims that can be checked, rather than asserted, are the ones that hold. Institutions adopting AI tools in education face a comparable test when they describe learning gains before anyone has measured them.

What would settle the Georgia pilot is a published record of curtailment events, independently verified, several years from now. Another announcement would not. Until then the programme remains a set of intentions with a number attached to it.
