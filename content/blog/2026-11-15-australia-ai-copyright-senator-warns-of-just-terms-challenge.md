Australian copyright law is being asked to carry an argument it was never designed for. Andrew McLachlan, a Liberal senator from South Australia, says that weakening copyright protections in order to attract artificial intelligence investment risks acquiring artists' property without just terms. That guarantee sits in section 51(xxxi) of the Constitution. [The Guardian's report on the senator's intervention](https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2026/nov/15/art-over-profit-ai-policy-debate-andrew-mclachlan) records that the criticism was aimed at both his own party and the Labor government. He has also lent support to possible constitutional challenges if legislation proceeds on that footing.

The practical question is narrower than the rhetoric. Labor's package, promised before the end of the year, is expected to address copyright, datacentres, online safety, energy costs and the economic benefits claimed for AI investment. Both major parties have tied themselves to datacentre growth. Anthropic has pressed for legal certainty and floated an opt-out model covering music, books and journalism, which would let rights holders withdraw material from training corpora rather than negotiate a licence in advance. Such a default places the evidential burden on the people whose work is used. Coalition senators Ross Cadell and Michaelia Cash have raised comparable property-rights objections. David Pocock has complained of a major-party arrangement that leaves crossbench scrutiny thin. Anthony Albanese has said creatives must be paid for their work, a commitment that sits awkwardly beside a package designed to speed investment.

For universities and schools, the consequences arrive through licensing and provenance rather than through classroom rules. A model trained on an opt-out corpus of published research will hold material whose rights status varies from title to title. Any institution that fine-tunes, licenses or evaluates such a system will need records robust enough to survive audit. Verifiable AI use in academic work depends on being able to say which text informed which output. A copyright settlement that leaves that question open simply moves the documentation burden from developers to the institutions using their tools.

Two developments will indicate whether the constitutional argument carries weight. The first is whether a challenge is filed at all. A just-terms claim turns on what the legislation takes and what compensation it offers, not on the force of the objection in principle. The second is the design of the new AI safety regulator and the six obligations on AI companies announced after a breach of government sites by OpenAI. A body with powers over training records would matter considerably more than one confined to incident reporting. Environmental groups opposing datacentre expansion on emissions and household power grounds have opened a second front. The end-of-year timetable forces each of these questions to a decision.
