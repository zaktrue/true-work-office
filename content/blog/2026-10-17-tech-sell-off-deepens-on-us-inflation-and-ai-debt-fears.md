Global equity markets fell sharply on 15 October 2026, with the heaviest losses concentrated in technology and chip shares. South Korea's Kospi 200 closed 8.1% lower, a six-month closing low that triggered circuit breakers, while Japan's Nikkei 225 lost 4.9%. On Wall Street the Nasdaq Composite had closed 1.9% down the previous session, with the S&P 500 off 1.1%, and losses extended into Thursday morning, led by Dell and Coherent. Losses were steepest among the companies closest to the AI trade: SK Hynix fell nearly 11%, Samsung Electronics 9.5%, and SoftBank, a major investor in OpenAI, 10%. [The Guardian's report on the sharp fall in technology and chip shares](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/oct/15/tech-market-sell-off-deepens-amid-fears-over-us-inflation-and-ai-debt) records Chinese, Taiwanese and European indices as declining too.

The immediate trigger was data rather than any single corporate event. US consumer price inflation reached 3.7% in September, the fastest rate since a 4.2% peak in May, while producer prices rose 6.3% year on year, the largest increase since December 2022. Deutsche Bank analysts, led by Jim Reid, noted that the rise was spread across the consumer basket rather than confined to energy costs shaped by geopolitical tensions, a distinction that matters because broad-based inflation is harder for central banks to look through. Investors are positioning for Federal Reserve interest rate increases, with some expecting one as soon as the end of October.

Behind the daily moves sits a structural concern that gives the sell-off public significance beyond portfolio damage. The International Monetary Fund has warned that a correction in AI-linked share prices could threaten global financial stability, with Asian markets judged most exposed after the United States, and UK and eurozone markets relatively insulated. That warning has arrived alongside growing scrutiny of heavy debt levels in the technology sector, where funding for compute infrastructure has far outpaced the returns documented to date.

The episode exposes a gap that matters well beyond investing. Financial exposure to AI is broad enough that a repricing of expectations transmits into pension funds, exporters and chip supply chains, which makes this a governance question as much as a market one: who assessed the downside, and against what evidence. The same discipline applies to deployment claims. In education, institutions asked to buy AI systems on the promise of transformative results are making a version of the same assessment, weighing documented capability against marketing. The sell-off does not measure whether the technology works; it measures how much was borrowed against the assumption that it will.

What remains unsettled is whether a repricing recalibrates expectations towards verifiable capability, or simply relocates the pressure to the next quarter's numbers.
