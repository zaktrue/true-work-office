San Francisco's economy has swung back from the pandemic years, and artificial intelligence is doing much of the pushing. [BBC's report on the city's AI revival and the correction fears gathering around it](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqe9k09j9jv4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) describes rising rents, falling office vacancies and a downtown recovering from the departure of remote workers. It also notes thousands of new start-ups and expensive engineering hires at OpenAI and Anthropic. Even a local dog-walking business serving AI firms has added staff and vehicles. The boom is visible enough to be counted in lease signings and job adverts.

The figures behind it are more uneven than the mood suggests. One cited study found that 60% of US AI venture capital between 2023 and 2025 went to firms based in the city. That concentration leaves San Francisco unusually exposed to any shift in sentiment. Yet the city remains roughly 35,000 jobs below its pre-pandemic peak, and much of the spending on AI infrastructure flows to data centres well outside the Bay Area. Capital arriving in San Francisco is not the same thing as employment staying there.

Warnings have arrived alongside the optimism. The International Monetary Fund cautioned that a sharp correction in AI share prices, and the fall in investment that could follow, is plausible. Nvidia's quarterly profits doubled year on year to $65bn, a result that sits awkwardly beside a governance scandal involving its chief executive, Jensen Huang. Two AI workers quoted anonymously disagree about what comes next. One expects collapse, because revenues may not justify the spending as cheaper Chinese models emerge. The other treats a correction as likely but ranks the technology's existential risk higher. Neither view is evidence, though both point at the same question.

That question is accountability. Ted Egan, a chief economist, expects a slowdown to be a hiccup rather than a structural break, and growth in healthcare and tourism may absorb part of the shock. The channels through which a correction would travel are more mundane than share prices: service jobs, empty offices, property values. A city that has staked its recovery on one industry has limited room to argue with that industry's balance sheet.

The same discipline applies wherever AI spending outruns independent evidence of benefit, including classrooms and university procurement, where adoption often precedes proof. San Francisco's experience tests whether a place can enjoy the returns of a concentrated industry without becoming hostage to its mood. Whether the correction comes or not, the more useful question is what the city has actually built, and who would carry the cost if the spending stopped.
